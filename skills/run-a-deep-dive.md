# Run a deep dive

## Purpose

Spend a day on one posting, and come out with a much bigger profile.

This is a **modifier on the normal per-application loop**, not a replacement. Every skill in that sequence still runs and every rule still holds. What changes is how much attention each stage gets, and that new atoms are expected to come out of it rather than being deferred.

## The argument for doing it

**The atoms are permanent. The application is not.**

A normal application takes twenty to forty minutes and produces one tailored document. A deep dive takes roughly a day and produces one tailored document *plus* a body of career evidence that did not previously exist in writing — new atoms, corrections to existing ones, skills that had been sitting on a resume with nothing behind them, work nobody had ever written down.

The cost is charged once. The return gets drawn on by every application afterward. Even if this particular posting goes nowhere — and it might — the excavation still paid, which is a thing almost nothing else in a job search can claim.

**The normal loop would not have found any of it, by design.** It asks only what the profile can't answer, in one bounded round, because that's correct when you're applying to a lot of things. A deep dive inverts the assumption: it treats the profile as incomplete, and treats the lines the posting cares most about as the place to dig.

## When to invoke

- **The posting is genuinely high-fit and worth an outsized shot.**
- ⚠️ **The profile is thin exactly where the posting is deep.** *This is the strongest single signal.* If the posting's core is three things the profile holds only as skills-line words with no stories behind them, that's the one. Not the posting they want most — the one where the gap between what they've done and what's written down is widest.
- **A stretch of their work has never been excavated.** Two years in a role that produced four atoms means there's more there.

## When not to

⛔ **Never for volume.** Under time pressure this produces one excellent application and no others. If they have six things to apply to this week, this is the wrong skill.

⛔ **Never on your own initiative.** It's a day of *their* attention, not yours. Propose it, explain the trade, let them decide.

⚠️ **Scope it before starting, or it will overrun.** It overruns anyway. Either agree explicitly that it runs across several sittings, or scope the interview to the posting's top three or four lines rather than all of them. Deciding this at the start is the difference between finishing and abandoning halfway with a half-excavated profile and no application.

## This is the session where new atoms are expected

Normally, a fact that surfaces mid-application isn't usable in that application — it gets mentioned, the application finishes, and the profile gets updated afterward. That rule exists because a claim invented under posting pressure must never harden into the permanent record.

**A deep dive is the exception, and the reason is worth being precise about.** What makes it safe isn't that it's a special session. It's that the person is present for every single fact, sourcing it themselves, in detail, slowly. The protection was never really about timing — it was about nobody checking. Here, they're checking.

Which means the exception evaporates the moment the pace picks up:

⛔ **If the session drifts toward throughput — batching questions, accepting quick answers, hurrying to get to the document — the normal rule is back in force**, regardless of what the session was opened as. A fast, hands-off session is exactly the condition that produces invented atoms. Speed is the hazard, not concurrency.

The consequence, which is the whole point: **facts surfaced here are usable in the application that surfaced them.** The normal loop forbids that, which means the deepest excavation would otherwise produce material this application can't touch. This session type exists to reverse that trade.

## The interview — the core of the method

### One line at a time, and you decide when it's done

⛔ **Not a batch of questions answered in one pass. Not "does that cover it?"**

Take one line from the posting. Ask about it. **Follow up until the answer couldn't be misread by a hiring manager.** Then move on to the next line.

⚠️ **You have to be willing to say *"that's still ambiguous"* to a real answer.** Accepting a plausible-but-vague answer to keep things moving is the exact failure this protocol exists to prevent, and it's tempting every time, because the person answered in good faith and pushing feels rude.

It works because compression hides things. A single paragraph about one tool, opened up properly, routinely contains half a dozen facts that were never in the profile — including the kind of hard adoption evidence that's almost impossible to get any other way. And a claim stated loosely in the first pass often gets **corrected by the person themselves a few minutes later**, once they've thought about it. A single pass would have banked the loose version as an atom.

### Announce the blocks up front

Structure it so they can see the shape and stop between sections. A workable order:

**A.** The role framing — what this job actually is.
**B.** Questions raised by outside research, including anything from the adjacent-posting check below.
**C.** The responsibilities, one at a time.
**D.** The qualification lists.

Say the blocks at the start. A day-long interview with no visible structure feels endless; the same interview with four named parts feels finite.

### Agree a continuation marker

Long answers get cut off — by message limits, by interruptions, by someone stopping to think. **Agree a marker up front** (`... more ...` works) that means *I'm not finished.*

⛔ **Never interpret a partial answer.** A session that responds to half a thought builds on half a fact, and the person then has to argue backwards out of a conclusion you drew from something they hadn't finished saying.

### Name every inference as an inference

**When a draft contains something they didn't actually say, say so before showing it.**

This starts out as a truth guard and turns into the best elicitation technique in the skill. Flagging *"this next part is my inference, you didn't tell me this"* does one of two things, and both are wins: they correct it, or — more often — they confirm it and then supply the real detail, which is invariably better and more specific than the inference was.

**Naming the gap invites them to fill it. Smoothing over it loses the best material in the session.**

## Four steps the normal loop doesn't have

### 1. Read the employer's other open postings for the same product

**Highest-value cheap step in this file.** Search the employer's careers page for anything posted alongside the target role.

What it gets you:

- **It kills false red flags.** A paragraph that reads as a worrying requirement often appears *verbatim* in three other postings, which means it's a template block and signals nothing about this role at all.
- **It reveals shape.** Two related roles posted together usually means the product is being invested in, and tells you something about where this role sits relative to the other.
- **It names things the target posting never mentions** — standards, compliance regimes, technologies — that are clearly part of the environment and are worth being ready for.

⛔ **Record these findings in their own file** — `applications/<slug>/posting-adjacent-<role>.md` — never merged into `posting.md`. That file is the record of the posting they applied to, and it stays clean.

⚠️ **A finding from early in a long session gets forgotten by the person who heard it.** Hours after establishing that some paragraph was boilerplate, they may reason from that very paragraph to make a decision. **Re-surfacing an earlier finding when a later decision contradicts it is part of the job** — nobody holds a full day of findings in their head, including you.

### 2. Verify claims about the employer, not just about the candidate

**The most important step here, and the easiest one to skip.**

Every other check in this system guards the person's own record. This one guards the **statements they make about the company** — and those are the ones the hiring team is uniquely equipped to catch, because it's their company.

If a cover letter or an application answer praises the employer for something, **go read the page it came from.** Not a summary of the page. The page.

The failure this prevents: complimenting a company on a quality its own published material contradicts. It's the most checkable sentence in the whole application, it reads as skimming rather than interest, and it's the precise opposite of the effect it was written for. A generic compliment is survivable. A specific compliment that's *wrong* is worse than nothing.

⚠️ **The instinct is usually right even when the fact is wrong.** If they wanted to praise the company's implementation approach and the actual page says something different, the answer isn't to cut the paragraph — it's to find what the page *does* support and say that instead. There's almost always something.

**Don't paraphrase a paraphrase.** Fetch it and read it.

### 3. Check whether the positioning line was chosen or inherited

The headline under someone's name — the line that says what they are — is very often there because of how a file was named, or what an old resume said, and **nobody ever decided it.**

Against a specific posting, ask the question directly: does this line lead with the word the posting is looking for?

The argument for keeping an existing line is usually real — it's their actual title, it's checkable, a qualifier in it is a genuine differentiator. But **the headline is the only line guaranteed to be read**, and in the few seconds it gets, it's being pattern-matched against the job title.

**Usually the answer is reorder, not replace.** *"Product Owner | Technical Business Analyst"* rather than the reverse keeps every advantage and puts the posting's word first. It costs nothing.

⛔ **Do not touch the job titles inside the experience section.** Those are factual title history, not positioning, and changing them is misrepresentation rather than emphasis.

### 4. Take the page-break check seriously

A deep dive produces more content than the normal loop, so it's the one most likely to overflow. See `skills/render-documents.md` → *Fix problems at the source* for the mechanics — in particular that trimming characters buys nothing unless a line disappears, and that a block splitting across a page break is usually fixed by trimming the line *above* it rather than cutting inside it.

## Failure modes this skill exists to catch

### The invented flourish — only they can catch it

**Distinct from a false fact.** This is a *true* fact given a vivid word the source never used. It survives review precisely because it reads well.

Something described in the source as *direct messages to team leads and emailed tickets* becomes *"hallway conversations"* in a draft. The fact is intact. The connotation isn't: one is a legitimate uncontrolled intake channel, the other is corridor chatter, and only one of those is a thing a hiring manager respects.

**The check: compare the drafted prose back against the atom's own wording, not just against the fact.** And hand these to the person explicitly — you cannot reliably catch your own flourishes, because they read well to you by construction.

### The true claim that mis-signals

**Harder to catch than an inaccuracy, because nothing is wrong.**

Someone considers labelling their independent work *"Business Development."* They genuinely do business development — finding clients, running demos, closing work. But "Business Development" is a hiring term of art that means *sales*, and on a product resume it reads as *salesperson*.

Same class: *"leadership wanted it, so we built it"* is true, and it answers "tell me about a decision you owned" with "someone above me decided."

**A true framing that aims the reader wrong costs more than a vague one.**

### Editorializing about a former employer

*"...a development organization that had none"* and similar constructions get written a lot, and the fault isn't the phrasing — it's the stance. Both point the reader at a former employer's inadequacy.

**"First" does the same work without the criticism.** And it's worth noticing that even *that* is often better cut: on a page, being first invites a question about the organization that no resume line can answer. It's excellent interview material and mediocre resume material.

⛔ **Never describe a manager or a former employer as not understanding something.**

### Register isn't settled by the voice file

A draft can pass every test in `profile/voice.md` — they'd say it out loud, it adds a fact, it's arguable — and still be **too much** for this particular audience.

**`voice.md` catches what's *wrong*. It doesn't catch what's *too much*.** Only the person can judge that, so show them the line and ask.

### Dangling pronouns

*"Part of it"* with no antecedent. *"Honored to be part of it"* with nothing named. These cluster in closing paragraphs, where the writing gets warm and vague at the same time.

**Every pronoun in a close needs a noun the reader can point at.** Worth checking your own drafts twice; this one is very easy to flag in someone else's writing and then commit immediately afterward.

## Where things get written

| File | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| `posting.md` | Normal intake, unchanged |
| `posting-adjacent-<role>.md` | One file per adjacent posting. **Never merged into `posting.md`** |
| `interview-notes.md` | **The working record.** Verbatim answers, the reasoning behind every choice, and everything cut — with the reason |
| `fit-analysis.md` | Normal, but written *after* the interview so it reflects the new atoms |
| `application-questions.md` | Answers, each with why that atom was chosen and what's being held back |
| `profile/achievements.md`, `profile/skills.md` | **Written during the session.** See above |

⚠️ **Bias toward keeping material, not toward tidiness.** Save as much as possible, *especially the things that don't quite fit the resume or cover letter* — that's where the interview answers come from. Anything cut for length, register, or relevance gets recorded **with the reason**, so they can overrule you rather than discover something quietly vanished.

## Answering application questions

Long application questions almost always ask for a specific example, and the pattern is reliable: **the person supplies the thinking and forgets to supply the example.**

This is where the deep dive pays off in an unexpected way. Because the atoms now exist, **the example is selected rather than invented**, and the selection is the actual judgment:

- **Budget the atoms across the questions.** One strong story each. Expect to reallocate mid-draft when a later question turns out to be a better home for a story you already spent.
- **Match the atom to what the question is really testing.** A question about delighting users wants a story where that's the whole point, not one where it trails a metric. A question about ownership needs an atom with a decision in it.
- **Record what's held in reserve, and why**, so a later question can reach for it.

## Guardrails

- **The truth rule is unchanged.** Writing atoms during the session is licensed by the person's attention, **not by convenience.** Every new atom traces to something they said, in this session, in detail.
- ⛔ **Internal system names never reach a document.** Expect several; a deep dive surfaces them constantly.
- ⛔ **Never name a client, a customer, or an individual** — including when the story reflects well on them.
- ⚠️ **Most people under-claim, and the hedges are where the best material is.** *"I did sort of know,"* *"I was a collaborator on it,"* *"it wasn't really me."* **Push on every one.** Entire specializations get recovered from a single hedge.
- ⚠️ **Run `skills/check-for-ai-tells.md` at the end.** It is very tempting to skip it after a day of line-by-line review, on the grounds that everything has already been read closely. Careful review is mitigation, not the pass — and this document has had more new prose written into it than any other.

## Done looks like

- The posting was interviewed line by line, not in one batch
- Adjacent postings were checked and recorded in their own file
- Every claim about the employer was verified against the employer's own page
- New atoms are in `profile/`, each traceable to something the person said this session
- `interview-notes.md` holds the cut material with reasons, not just the kept material
- The tells pass ran
- They know the profile got bigger, and roughly by how much — that's the return on the day
